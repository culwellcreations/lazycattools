If you and I both measure "1 cup of flour", there's a good chance we end up with different amounts. Mine might be 120 g. Yours might be 145 g. Neither of us did anything wrong. We just filled the cup differently.

That gap is one of the most common reasons a recipe that works for the person who wrote it turns out dry, dense or crumbly for everyone else. Flour packs down easily, so the way it gets into the cup decides how much you're really using.

## Dip and sweep

You push the measuring cup into the bag, pull it out heaped and scrape the top flat. It's quick and it's how a lot of people learned. It also compresses the flour, so a cup usually weighs somewhere around 135 to 145 g. America's Test Kitchen uses this method on purpose, which is why their recipes count a cup of all-purpose flour as 5 oz (142 g).

## Spoon and level

You stir the flour to loosen it, spoon it into the cup until it's heaped, then sweep a straight edge across the top. Done this way, a cup weighs about 120 g. This is the method King Arthur Baking and most American recipe writers assume, and it's the number used across this site.

## Weigh it

You put the bowl on a scale, press tare and add flour until the display says the number the recipe wants. 120 g is 120 g, every time, in every kitchen.

## Spooning and leveling, step by step

1. Stir or whisk the flour in its bag or canister first. Flour settles during shipping and storage, and settled flour is heavier.
2. Use a dry measuring cup, the kind with a flat rim, not a glass jug with lines on the side.
3. Spoon the flour in loosely until it mounds above the rim. Don't press it down.
4. Don't tap the cup on the counter or shake it to level it. Both pack the flour.
5. Sweep the back of a knife across the rim to scrape off the extra.

It's slower than scooping, and it's still less consistent than a scale, but it gets you close to what the recipe writer meant.

## "1 cup flour, sifted" is not the same as "1 cup sifted flour"

Read where the word sifted sits. "1 cup flour, sifted" means measure the flour first, then sift it. That's still about 120 g. "1 cup sifted flour" means sift first, then spoon the sifted flour into the cup. Sifting adds a lot of air, so that cup weighs noticeably less.

Older American cookbooks often call for sifted flour. If a vintage cake recipe keeps coming out heavy, this is the first thing I'd check.

## What about other flours?

Bread flour weighs about the same as all-purpose by the cup. Cake flour is a little lighter, around 113 g. Almond flour is lighter again by the spoonful but varies a lot between brands. The [kitchen charts](/kitchen/) list each one with where the number comes from.

## Honestly, just weigh it

I'd rather tell you this plainly than pretend the spoon method is as good. A basic digital kitchen scale costs less than a nice baking pan, and it makes flour the easiest ingredient in the recipe instead of the most error-prone. You also wash fewer measuring cups.
