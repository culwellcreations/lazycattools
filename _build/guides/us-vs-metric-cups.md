A cup sounds like one of the few measurements everyone agrees on. It isn't. Depending on where a recipe was written, "1 cup" can mean anything from 200 ml to 284 ml. Most of the time the difference is small enough to ignore. Sometimes it isn't.

## The cups you'll run into

| Cup | Volume | Where you'll see it |
|---|---|---|
| US customary cup | 236.6 ml | American recipes and measuring sets |
| US "legal" cup | 240 ml | Nutrition labels in the US |
| Metric cup | 250 ml | Australia, New Zealand, Canada, and many UK recipes that use cups |
| Imperial cup | 284 ml | Older British recipes (half an imperial pint) |
| Japanese cup | 200 ml | Japanese recipes (a rice cooker cup is 180 ml) |

Every converter and chart on this site uses the US customary cup, because that's what most measuring cups sold in the US hold.

## How much does the difference matter?

A metric cup holds about 6% more than a US cup. For most recipes that's well inside the normal wobble of measuring by volume. Scooping flour instead of spooning it changes the amount by 15 to 20%, which is a much bigger error than the cup size.

Where it starts to matter is when a recipe uses a lot of cups of the same thing. Five metric cups of flour in a bread recipe is about 65 ml more than five US cups. That's enough to make dough noticeably stiffer.

The old imperial cup is a bigger jump, about 20% more than a US cup. If you're baking from a British cookbook printed before the 1970s and it gives cups, that's probably what it means.

## Measuring other cups with US cups

If you only have US measuring cups, you can get very close to the others with a spoon:

- For 1 metric cup (250 ml), use 1 US cup plus 1 tablespoon. That's 251 ml.
- For 1 imperial cup (284 ml), use 1 US cup plus 3 tablespoons. That's 281 ml.
- For 1 Japanese cup (200 ml), use ¾ cup plus 1 tablespoon and 2 teaspoons. That's 202 ml.

## Spoons are where it gets risky

Teaspoons are 5 ml almost everywhere (a US teaspoon is 4.9 ml, close enough). Tablespoons are 15 ml in the US, the UK, Canada and New Zealand.

Australia is the exception. An Australian tablespoon is 20 ml, which is 4 teaspoons instead of 3. For flour or sugar that hardly matters. For baking soda, salt or a strong spice, using a US tablespoon in an Australian recipe means you're putting in 25% less than the writer intended. If an Australian recipe calls for 1 tablespoon of baking powder, use 1 tablespoon plus 1 teaspoon.

## Fluid ounces and pints aren't the same either

A US fluid ounce is 29.6 ml and a UK fluid ounce is 28.4 ml. The bigger trap is the pint. A US pint is 16 fluid ounces (473 ml), while a UK pint is 20 UK fluid ounces (568 ml). A British recipe calling for a pint of milk wants about 20% more than an American pint.

## How to tell which cup a recipe means

Look at where it comes from. A website ending in .com.au or .co.nz almost certainly uses metric cups. So does a recipe that also lists oven temperatures in Celsius and butter in grams.

If you really can't tell, the safest thing is to find a version of the recipe that lists weights. Grams mean the same thing everywhere, which is the whole reason most of the world switched to them. When a recipe gives both cups and grams, trust the grams.
