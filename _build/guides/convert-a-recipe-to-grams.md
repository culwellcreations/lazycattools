Converting a recipe to grams takes about ten minutes the first time. After that, the recipe comes out the same every time and leaves fewer measuring cups in the sink.

## 1. Get a scale and learn the tare button

Any digital kitchen scale that reads in 1 g steps is enough. The button you'll use most is tare (sometimes labeled zero). Put the bowl on the scale, press tare, and the display goes back to 0 so you only weigh what goes in. Add the flour, press tare again, add the sugar, and so on. One bowl, no cups.

## 2. Convert each ingredient by what it is

This is the step people get wrong. There's no single number of grams in a cup, because different ingredients weigh different amounts. A cup of flour is about 120 g, a cup of sugar about 200 g, a cup of honey about 340 g. Any chart that gives one number for everything is giving you the weight of water.

Here's a typical chocolate chip cookie recipe converted:

| Recipe says | Grams |
|---|---|
| 2¼ cups all-purpose flour | 270 g |
| 1 cup butter | 227 g |
| ¾ cup granulated sugar | 150 g |
| ¾ cup packed brown sugar | 160 g |
| 2 cups chocolate chips | 340 g |
| 1 tsp baking soda | 1 tsp (4.8 g) |
| 1 tsp salt | 1 tsp (6 g) |
| 2 large eggs | 2 eggs |

You can paste a whole ingredient list into the [recipe converter](/kitchen/recipe-converter/) and it does every line at once, using the weight of each ingredient. Anything it doesn't know is left alone and marked, so you can look that one up.

## 3. Leave the small stuff in spoons

Baking soda, baking powder, salt, yeast and spices usually come in amounts under 10 g. Most kitchen scales only read whole grams, so trying to weigh 4.8 g of baking soda on one is less accurate than using a level teaspoon. Keep these in spoons unless you have a small scale that reads to 0.1 g.

## 4. Round to numbers you can actually hit

Nobody needs 227 g of butter written on a recipe card when 225 g will do. For anything over 100 g, rounding to the nearest 5 g is fine. For smaller amounts, round to the nearest gram. The one exception is bread, where water and flour work as a ratio. Keep those exact if you can.

## 5. Bake it once and check

The recipe you're converting was written by someone who measured cups their own way. If they scooped their flour, their "cup" may have been closer to 140 g than 120 g, and the converted recipe will be a little softer or runnier than you're used to.

So bake the converted version once and compare. If it's wetter than usual, add flour 10 g at a time next time. Then write the final numbers on the card and stop thinking about it.

## A few things that help

Eggs stay as eggs. A large egg is about 50 g without the shell, which is useful if you're scaling a recipe and end up needing half of one.

Liquids can be weighed too. Water is 1 g per ml, and milk is close (a cup is about 244 g). Pouring milk straight into the bowl on the scale is easier than reading the line on a jug.

Sticky things like honey, peanut butter and syrup are where weighing pays off fastest. Spoon them straight into the bowl until the scale hits the number, and you skip scraping out a measuring cup.
